# Language and Meaning

A selective paraphrase of the second edition's language guidance. Main locations: Unit 1 pp. 53-68; Unit 3 pp. 183-187; Unit 4 pp. 229-241; Unit 8 pp. 318-329. The review questions are an operational restatement, not a copied checklist.

## Tense

Choose tense in relation to what the sentence means in context:

- Past simple often locates an action or observation in a completed study.
- Present simple can describe the current paper or a displayed figure and can present information as established or generally applicable.
- Present perfect can connect previous work or an unresolved issue to the present state of knowledge.

These are interpretive tendencies, not automatic substitution rules. Different functions within the Results may require different tenses. Present tense does not by itself establish general truth, and changing tense cannot strengthen evidence.

## Voice and Ownership

Make clear whether a statement describes established background, another study, the present work or an interpretation. Passive voice can be appropriate, but not when it obscures a distinction the reader needs. Active voice is not universally better.

Locate citations so that the reader can identify which statement belongs to the cited source. Do not blur the present contribution with earlier work.

## Sentence Connections

Begin with a concept or object the reader can identify, then introduce the new information. The next sentence can develop that information explicitly.

Check whether this, these, it and which have clear referents. Repeat a technical term when a synonym would introduce ambiguity. Use a connecting word only when it expresses the actual relationship between statements.

## Paragraphs

Plan what a paragraph is doing for the reader. Several functions can coexist within one paragraph, but its main purpose and development should remain apparent.

Move or connect sentences that abruptly change the task. Do not use example sentence or paragraph lengths as universal acceptance thresholds.

## Certainty and Modality

Distinguish a reported observation from an explanation or inference. The wording should preserve the intended degree and kind of certainty.

Can, may, might, could, should and must express context-dependent meanings such as capability, possibility, expectation, recommendation and obligation. They are not interchangeable positions on a numerical confidence scale.

Do not weaken an adequately supported statement merely to sound cautious. Conversely, a hedge is not a substitute for a reasonable basis for an interpretation.

## Precision and Ambiguity

Inspect noun chains, relative clauses, strings of prepositions and unanchored participial clauses. Identify which object each modifier describes and who performs each action.

Check the placement of only and the reference of respectively. Preserve distinctions in quantities and comparisons, such as increased by versus increased to.

Split a sentence when it contains competing main messages or obscures relationships, not simply because it passes an arbitrary word limit.

## Practical Review

Ask whether the revised passage preserves its original proposition, communicates its function, connects to surrounding text, identifies ownership, uses appropriate certainty and avoids new ambiguity. These questions put the book's principles into use; they do not validate the underlying research.
